Solar Power Basics

A solar panel turns sunlight into electricity using photovoltaic cells made mostly from silicon. When light hits a cell, it knocks electrons loose and creates a flow of direct current. An inverter then changes that direct current into alternating current, which is the type used by household appliances.

Most home panels convert between 15 and 22 percent of the sunlight they receive into electricity. Panel efficiency drops slowly over time, and manufacturers usually guarantee that a panel will still produce at least 80 percent of its original output after 25 years. Heat reduces output, so panels on a roof with good airflow perform better in hot weather.

A typical home system has between 15 and 25 panels and produces about 6 kilowatts at peak sunlight. The best direction for panels in the northern hemisphere is south, with a tilt close to the latitude of the location. Shade from trees or chimneys can cut the output of a whole string of panels, so installers often add small devices called microinverters to limit the loss.

Batteries store extra electricity for use at night. A common home battery holds about 10 kilowatt hours, which can run a refrigerator, lights, and a router for roughly a full day. Without a battery, a home usually sends extra power to the grid and receives credit from the utility company.

The payback period for a home solar system is often between 6 and 10 years, depending on electricity prices and local incentives. Regular maintenance is simple: rinse dust off the panels a few times a year and check that the inverter display shows no error.
